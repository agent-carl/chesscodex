The Tarrasch Variation of the [French Defense](/openings/french-defense) (1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.Nd2) is named after Siegbert Tarrasch. White defends e4 with the queen's knight from d2 instead of c3, a less aggressive choice than 3.Nc3 that is still used by top players looking for a small, safe advantage.

### Origins

The Tarrasch became particularly popular in the 1970s and early 1980s, when Anatoly Karpov used it to great effect. The line 3...c5 4.exd5 exd5 was a staple of the Karpov–Korchnoi battles, including seven games of their 1974 Candidates match.

### The idea

Unlike 3.Nc3, the knight on d2 does not block the c-pawn, so White can support d4 with c3. That also takes the sting out of the Winawer, since 3...Bb4 can be met by 4.c3. On the other hand, the knight is on a less active square and shuts in the dark-squared bishop, so White usually has to spend a tempo moving it before developing the bishop.

### Main lines

- 3...c5, the [Open System](/openings/french-defense-tarrasch-variation-open-system):
  - 4.exd5 exd5, usually leaving Black with an isolated queen's pawn. The main line runs 5.Ngf3 Nc6 6.Bb5 Bd6 7.0-0 Nge7 8.dxc5 Bxc5 9.Nb3 Bb6; if White neutralises Black's active pieces, White has a slight advantage in the ending;
  - 4.exd5 Qxd5, the [Chistyakov Defense](/openings/french-defense-tarrasch-variation-chistyakov-defense), trading Black's c- and d-pawns for White's d- and e-pawns to get an extra centre pawn, in return for losing time with the queen;
  - 4.Ngf3, the [Euwe-Keres Line](/openings/french-defense-tarrasch-variation-open-system-euwe-keres-line), played by Paul Keres and Max Euwe;
  - 4.c3 ([Süchting Line](/openings/french-defense-tarrasch-variation-open-system-suchting-line)) gives White no advantage after 4...cxd4 5.cxd4 dxe4 6.Nxe4 Nf6.
- 3...Nf6, the [Closed Variation](/openings/french-defense-tarrasch-variation-closed-variation), aiming to close the centre: 4.e5 Nfd7 5.Bd3 c5 6.c3 Nc6 7.Ne2 cxd4 8.cxd4 ([main line](/openings/french-defense-tarrasch-variation-closed-variation-main-line)) f6, freeing Black's pieces at the cost of a backward e6-pawn. 5.f4 is the [Pawn Center Variation](/openings/french-defense-tarrasch-variation-pawn-center-variation).
- 3...Be7, the [Morozevich Variation](/openings/french-defense-tarrasch-variation-morozevich-variation), a fashionable waiting move among top grandmasters.
- 3...Nc6, the [Guimard Defense](/openings/french-defense-tarrasch-variation-guimard-defense); White should keep a slight advantage.
- 3...dxe4, entering the [Rubinstein Variation](/openings/french-defense-rubinstein-variation) and avoiding Tarrasch-specific theory.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Avoids the Winawer and keeps the option of c3.
- Solid, aiming for a small, safe advantage.

**Weaknesses**

- The knight on d2 blocks the dark-squared bishop.
- Less aggressive than 3.Nc3.

### Who should play it

The Tarrasch suits White players who want a solid, positional answer to the French without facing the Winawer.

### The numbers

On Lichess White leads at every level: 52.4% against 44.4% under 1400, 52.4% against 43.6% at 1400–1799, 50.7% against 43.8% at 1800–2199 and 48.7% against 43.3% from 2200 up. In about 43,700 master games White won 35.7%, Black 22.2%, and 42.1% were drawn.
