The Blackburne Shilling Gambit, called the Blackburne-Kostić Gambit on this site, is 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Nd4, a dubious offshoot of the [Italian Game](/openings/italian-game). The name is facetious. The line is also called the Kostić Gambit after the Serbian grandmaster Borislav Kostić, who played it in the early 20th century.

### History

Wilhelm Steinitz made the first known mention of the line in 1895. The legend behind the name is that the English master Joseph Henry Blackburne used its trap to win a shilling a game from café visitors, but there are no recorded games of Blackburne playing it.

### The trap

The only virtue of 3...Nd4 is its trap. After the natural 4.Nxe5? Black wins material with 4...Qg5! The obvious 5.Nxf7 then loses to 5...Qxg2 6.Rf1 Qxe4+ 7.Be2 Nf3#, a smothered mate.

The line is often said not to be a true gambit, since White cannot take on e5 without losing material. Still, after 4.Nxe5 Qg5 White can keep a playable game with 5.Bxf7+!, getting two pawns and an attack for the piece.

### How White should answer

3...Nd4 is a weak, time-wasting move. Steinitz recommended 4.O-O or 4.Nxd4. Jeremy Silman writes that White is better after 4.O-O, 4.c3 or 4.Nc3, and recommends as best 4.Nxd4 exd4 5.c3. After 5...Bc5 Black loses a pawn to 6.Bxf7+ Kxf7 7.Qh5+. Graham Burgess notes that if the trap is avoided, White is left with a large advantage.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 48.6% of the 4.3 million games and Black 47.3%. Under 1400 Black wins 51.3% against 45.5%. From 2200 up White leads 49.7% to 45.0%. Masters over the board almost never play it; the database has two games.
