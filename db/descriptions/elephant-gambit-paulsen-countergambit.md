The Paulsen Countergambit is 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d5 3.exd5 e4, the main way for Black to continue the [Elephant Gambit](/openings/elephant-gambit) after White takes on d5. Instead of regaining the pawn, Black pushes the e-pawn and attacks the knight on f3. The Elephant Gambit as a whole is considered unsound: Black can end up in a passive position, with White holding the initiative and more space.

### After 4.Qe2

White's usual answer is 4.Qe2, pinning the e-pawn. After 4...Nf6 play can go:

- 5.d3 Qxd5 6.Nbd2 Be7 7.dxe4 Qe6, when White remains a pawn ahead, although Black's development is somewhat smoother.
- 5.d3 Be7 6.dxe4 O-O 7.Nc3 Re8 8.Bd2 Bb4 9.O-O-O, with advantage for White according to Nick de Firmian.
- 5.Nc3 Be7 6.Nxe4. After 6...Nxd5 7.d3 O-O 8.Qd1 Bg4 9.Be2 f5 10.Ng3 Nc6 11.c3 White has a slight advantage; after 6...O-O 7.Nxf6+ Bxf6 8.d4 Re8 9.Be3 de Firmian assesses White's position as distinctly superior.

### A game by Tal

Tal–Lutikov, Tallinn 1964, went 4.Qe2 f5 5.d3 Nf6 6.dxe4 fxe4 7.Nc3 Bb4 8.Qb5+ c6 9.Qxb4 exf3 10.Bg5 cxd5 11.O-O-O Nc6, with advantage for White.

### The numbers

On Lichess Black wins 51.4% of the 3.2 million games and White 44.6%. From 2200 up the gap narrows to 47.7% against 46.0%. There are only 69 master games, too few to judge.
