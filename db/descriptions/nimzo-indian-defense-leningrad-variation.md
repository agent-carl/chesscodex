The Leningrad Variation is 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Bb4 4.Bg5: against the [Nimzo-Indian Defense](/openings/nimzo-indian-defense) White pins the f6-knight in return. It got its name because players from Leningrad, such as Boris Spassky, developed much of its theory. Its ECO codes are E30 and E31.

### The idea

Instead of 4.e3 or 4.Qc2, White develops the dark-squared bishop at once and pins the knight on f6. In the main lines Black answers with ...c5 and, after White's d5, sets up a blockade on the dark squares.

### Main lines

Black can play 4...h6 or the immediate 4...c5. After 4...h6 the game may go 5.Bh4 c5 6.d5.

- 6...d6 7.e3 Bxc3+ 8.bxc3 e5 is the main line. Black has a blockade like the one in the Hübner Variation of the [Rubinstein System](/openings/nimzo-indian-defense-rubinstein-system), but White's dark-squared bishop is outside the pawn chain. The pin on the f6-knight is very annoying, and Black often breaks it with the drastic ...g7–g5, which also stops White's f2–f4 break. Because this weakens the kingside, Black usually keeps the king safe on the other side with ...Kd8–c7 instead of castling.
- 6...b5 was much played in the 1970s, after Mikhail Tal scored a crushing win over Spassky with it at Tallinn 1973.

### The numbers

On Lichess Black wins slightly more often overall: 48.0% against 47.0% for White in 1.7 million games. White is ahead below 1800, 52.2% against 44.4% under 1400, but from 1800 up Black leads: 48.8% against 45.9% between 1800 and 2199, and 48.0% against 45.3% from 2200 up. In 2,095 master games over the board White won 29.2%, Black 30.5%, and 40.2% were drawn.
