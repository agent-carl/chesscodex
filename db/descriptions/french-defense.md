The French Defense (1.e4 e6) is a solid, counterattacking answer to 1.e4. Black prepares 2...d5 and challenges White's centre immediately, accepting a slightly cramped position in return for a sturdy pawn chain and clear plans.

### Origins

The name comes from a correspondence match between London and Paris in 1834, in which the Paris team adopted 1...e6 on the advice of Jacques Chamouillet. In the early 20th century Géza Maróczy made it his main weapon against 1.e4. After the First World War Aron Nimzowitsch and Mikhail Botvinnik popularised 3...Bb4, and later Tigran Petrosian, Viktor Korchnoi and Wolfgang Uhlmann were among its leading players.
### The idea

After 2.d4 d5 the typical structure arises when White pushes e4–e5. White gets more space on the kingside, and Black's pawns stand on e6 and d5. Black then attacks the base of White's chain with ...c5, and sometimes the head of it with ...f6. The well-known problem piece is Black's light-squared bishop on c8, which is shut in by its own pawn on e6. Freeing it, or trading it with ...b6 and ...Ba6, is a recurring theme.

### Main lines

- [Advance Variation](/openings/french-defense-advance-variation) 3.e5: White grabs space, and Black attacks d4 with ...c5, ...Nc6 and ...Qb6.
- [Winawer Variation](/openings/french-defense-winawer-variation) 3.Nc3 Bb4: the sharpest line, with double-edged fights such as the [Poisoned Pawn](/openings/french-defense-winawer-variation-poisoned-pawn-variation).
- [Classical Variation](/openings/french-defense-classical-variation) 3.Nc3 Nf6, including the [Steinitz](/openings/french-defense-classical-variation-steinitz-variation) 4.e5.
- [Tarrasch Variation](/openings/french-defense-tarrasch-variation) 3.Nd2: a flexible, positional choice that avoids the Winawer.
- [Rubinstein Variation](/openings/french-defense-rubinstein-variation) 3...dxe4: a solid line that gives up the centre for easy development.
- [Exchange Variation](/openings/french-defense-exchange-variation) 3.exd5: symmetrical and often drawish.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Clear plans in almost every line: attack the pawn chain with ...c5 and ...f6.
- Solid structure: Black's king is rarely in immediate danger.
- Many lines are strategic rather than purely theoretical, so understanding counts for more than memory.

**Weaknesses**

- The "bad" light-squared bishop and the lack of space can leave Black passive.
- Black must be ready for very different systems: the Advance, Winawer and Tarrasch have little in common.
- The Exchange Variation lets White steer towards a dry, equal position.

### Who should play it

The French suits players who like closed positions, pawn structures and counterattack, and who don't mind defending for a while. It works well against aggressive 1.e4 players who overextend with e5 and f4, because the centre often becomes a target. Players who hate being cramped, or who want open piece play, usually prefer the [Sicilian](/openings/sicilian-defense) or the [Caro-Kann](/openings/caro-kann-defense). The Caro-Kann has a similar pawn structure but keeps the c8-bishop free.

### The numbers

On Lichess the French is close to even from beginner to advanced level: White wins 48% and Black 47–48%. Above 2200, White edges ahead 48% to 44.5%. In master games over the board, White wins 35% and Black 24%, with 41% draws.
