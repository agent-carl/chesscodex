The Morphy Defense (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 a6) is Black's most popular reply to the [Ruy Lopez](/openings/ruy-lopez), played in about two thirds of all Ruy Lopez games. With 3...a6 Black asks the bishop at once whether it wants to take on c6 or retreat.

### Origins

The move is named after Paul Morphy, although he did not invent it. He played it in the second game (a draw) and the fourth game (a win) of his 1858 match against Adolf Anderssen in Paris, and had met it earlier that year against Johann Löwenthal in London. Charles Henry Stanley had played 3...a6 twice in his 1845 match against Eugène Rousseau in New Orleans, losing both games but winning the match, and Howard Staunton mentioned it in his *Chess-Player's Handbook* (1847). The first author to mention it was Ercole del Rio, in his 1750 treatise. Wilhelm Steinitz disapproved of the move, writing in 1889 that it drives the bishop to a better square, but his opinion did not prevail.

### The idea

3...a6 "puts the question" to the bishop, a phrase attributed to Aron Nimzowitsch: White must exchange on c6 or retreat. After the usual retreat 4.Ba4, Black can later break the pin on the c6-knight with ...b5. The main line continues 4...Nf6, Morphy's own choice, and 5.O-O, which leaves the e4-pawn undefended because White can win it back if Black takes.

### Main lines

- 4.Bxc6, the [Exchange Variation](/openings/ruy-lopez-exchange-variation).
- 4.Ba4 Nf6 [5.O-O](/openings/ruy-lopez-morphy-defense-2), then:
  - 5...Be7, the solid [Closed Defense](/openings/ruy-lopez-closed), the most common choice;
  - 5...Nxe4, the [Open Defense](/openings/ruy-lopez-open);
  - 5...b5 6.Bb3 Bb7, the [Arkhangelsk Variation](/openings/ruy-lopez-morphy-defense-arkhangelsk-variation);
  - 5...Bc5, the [Neo-Arkhangelsk Variation](/openings/ruy-lopez-morphy-defense-neo-arkhangelsk-variation);
  - 5...d6, the [Steinitz Deferred](/openings/ruy-lopez-morphy-defense-steinitz-deferred).
- White's fifth-move alternatives include 5.d3 ([Anderssen Variation](/openings/ruy-lopez-morphy-defense-anderssen-variation)), 5.Qe2 ([Wormald Attack](/openings/ruy-lopez-morphy-defense-wormald-attack)), 5.Nc3 ([Tarrasch Variation](/openings/ruy-lopez-morphy-defense-tarrasch-variation)) and 5.d4 ([Mackenzie Variation](/openings/ruy-lopez-morphy-defense-mackenzie-variation)).
- Black's fourth-move alternatives include 4...d6, the [Modern Steinitz Defense](/openings/ruy-lopez-morphy-defense-modern-steinitz-defense), [4...Bc5](/openings/ruy-lopez-morphy-defense-classical-defense-deferred), and 4...b5 5.Bb3 Na5, the [Norwegian Variation](/openings/ruy-lopez-morphy-defense-norwegian-variation).

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Black keeps the option of ...b5 to break the pin and gain space on the queenside.
- It leads to the main lines of the Ruy Lopez, from the solid Closed Defense to the Open and Arkhangelsk systems.

**Weaknesses**

- The amount of theory, especially in the Closed Defense, is very large.
- Steinitz's objection: 3...a6 drives White's bishop to a better square.

### Who should play it

The Morphy Defense is the standard choice for Black players who meet 1.e4 with 1...e5 and are ready to learn the main lines of the Ruy Lopez.

### The numbers

On Lichess White scores well at every level: 52.4% against 43.2% under 1400, 50.9% against 44.6% at 1400–1799, 49.9% against 44.6% at 1800–2199, and 48.1% against 44.0% from 2200 up. In about 115,800 master games 50.9% were drawn; White won 29.8% and Black 19.3%.
