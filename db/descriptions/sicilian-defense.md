The Sicilian Defense (1.e4 c5) is Black's most popular and most ambitious answer to 1.e4. Instead of copying White with 1...e5, Black fights for the d4-square from the side, which leads to unbalanced positions where both players can play for a win.

### Origins

The move 1...c5 was analysed by the Italian Giulio Polerio as early as 1594, and the English name took hold in the early 19th century from Italian sources. For a long time it was considered dubious. It became a main-line defence only in the 20th century, and today it is the backbone of the repertoires of Bobby Fischer, Garry Kasparov and most modern elite players.

### The idea

After 2.Nf3 and 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 (the Open Sicilian), Black has traded a wing pawn for White's centre pawn. That gives Black an extra central pawn, the half-open c-file and long-term endgame chances. White gets a lead in development and more space, and often castles queenside to attack. The result is a race: White attacks the king, while Black counterattacks on the queenside and down the c-file.

### Main lines

The Open Sicilian branches according to Black's second move:

- **2...d6**: the [Najdorf](/openings/sicilian-defense-najdorf-variation) (5...a6), the sharpest and most respected line; the [Dragon](/openings/sicilian-defense-dragon-variation) (5...g6); the [Scheveningen](/openings/sicilian-defense-scheveningen-variation) (…e6 and …d6).
- **2...Nc6**: the [Sveshnikov](/openings/sicilian-defense-lasker-pelikan-variation-sveshnikov-variation), where Black accepts a hole on d5 in return for activity, and the [Accelerated Dragon](/openings/sicilian-defense-accelerated-dragon).
- **2...e6**: the flexible [Taimanov](/openings/sicilian-defense-taimanov-variation) and [Kan](/openings/sicilian-defense-kan-variation).

White can also avoid the main theory with an "Anti-Sicilian": the [Alapin](/openings/sicilian-defense-alapin-variation) (2.c3), the [Closed Sicilian](/openings/sicilian-defense-closed) (2.Nc3), the [Grand Prix Attack](/openings/sicilian-defense-grand-prix-attack), the [Rossolimo](/openings/sicilian-defense-nyezhmetdinov-rossolimo-attack) (2...Nc6 3.Bb5) or the [Smith-Morra Gambit](/openings/sicilian-defense-smith-morra-gambit) (2.d4 cxd4 3.c3).

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Black plays for a win from the first move, and the positions are rarely symmetrical or dry.
- It is objectively sound at every level: the Najdorf has been tested in world championship matches for more than 50 years.
- There are several families of lines to choose from, from sharp (Dragon, Najdorf) to positional (Taimanov, Kan).

**Weaknesses**

- The Open Sicilian is the most heavily analysed opening in chess. In sharp lines one forgotten move can lose on the spot.
- Black must be ready for many different Anti-Sicilians, each with its own ideas.
- Black's king often stays in the centre for a while, so a lack of care is punished quickly.

### Who should play it

The Sicilian suits players who like dynamic, double-edged positions, who are willing to learn concrete theory and who prefer to take risks for winning chances. Players who want a quiet life against 1.e4 are usually happier with the [Caro-Kann](/openings/caro-kann-defense) or 1...e5.

It is especially effective against 1.e4 players who want a standard open game, because it takes them out of their comfort zone. It is least comfortable against well-prepared Anti-Sicilians: the Alapin and the Rossolimo are chosen at top level precisely because they reduce Black's winning chances.

### The numbers

On Lichess the Sicilian scores almost exactly even at every rating level: White wins about 47–48% and Black 45–49%. Among masters playing over the board, draws rise to about 43%. White scores 31% and Black 26%, which is one of the better results for Black among the main replies to 1.e4.
