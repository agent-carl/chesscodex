The Queen's Gambit Accepted (1.d4 d5 2.c4 dxc4) is Black's most direct answer to the [Queen's Gambit](/openings/queens-gambit): Black takes the pawn. The key to playing it well is not to hold onto the pawn, but to use the time White spends winning it back to strike at the centre with ...c5 or ...e5.

### Origins

The Queen's Gambit Accepted is mentioned in chess literature as early as the 15th century. For a long time Black's play centred on holding on to the c4-pawn. The first modern ideas came in the 1886 World Championship match between Wilhelm Steinitz and Johannes Zukertort: Steinitz returned the pawn, left White with an isolated pawn on d4 and then played against that weakness. Even with this treatment the opening had a slightly dubious reputation in the early 20th century. Alexander Alekhine introduced further ideas for Black, and from the 1930s it was played at the highest level. It became less popular after the Second World War, when the Indian defences were heavily played. At the end of the 1990s a number of elite players added it to their repertoires, and today it is considered sound.

### The idea

After 2...dxc4 White has two main approaches:

- **Recover the pawn calmly** with Nf3, e3 and Bxc4, keeping a slight lead in development.
- **Take the centre** with 3.e4, the [Central Variation](/openings/queens-gambit-accepted-saduleto-variation), and then Bxc4.

Black then plays ...Nf6, ...e6, ...c5 and often ...a6 and ...b5, gaining space on the queenside. The typical result is an isolated d4-pawn for White (after ...cxd4 exd4), with active pieces against Black's solid blockade.

### Main lines

- [Normal Variation](/openings/queens-gambit-accepted-normal-variation) 3.Nf3, leading to the [Classical Defense](/openings/queens-gambit-accepted-classical-defense) 3...Nf6 4.e3 e6 5.Bxc4 c5, the main line at master level.
- [Old Variation](/openings/queens-gambit-accepted-old-variation) 3.e3: White recovers the pawn with the bishop. The immediate 3...e5 is a good answer.
- Central Variation 3.e4, answered by 3...e5, 3...Nf6 ([Alekhine System](/openings/queens-gambit-accepted-central-variation-alekhine-system)) or 3...c5.
- [Alekhine Defense](/openings/queens-gambit-accepted-alekhine-defense) 3.Nf3 a6, preparing ...b5 in the right moment.

### A common mistake

Beginners often try to protect c4 with ...b5. After a4 and sometimes Qf3 or Nc3, the queenside pawns collapse and White wins material back with interest. The lesson of the QGA is that the pawn on c4 is a loan, not a gift.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Clear, principled play that frees Black's game quickly: the c8-bishop is not shut in.
- Less theory than the [Queen's Gambit Declined](/openings/queens-gambit-declined) or the [Slav](/openings/slav-defense).
- Positions with an isolated d-pawn teach valuable middlegame skills.

**Weaknesses**

- White gets a free hand in the centre, especially after 3.e4.
- Players who don't know the ideas suffer: trying to keep the pawn is punished quickly.
- Many lines are symmetrical and lead to small but lasting edges for White.

### Who should play it

The QGA suits players who want active piece play and a straightforward plan against 1.d4, and who are comfortable in open positions. It is a good choice against opponents who aim for slow positional squeezes, because the centre opens early. It is less comfortable against players who like the 3.e4 lines and big centres.

### The numbers

Below 1800 on Lichess, White wins 55–56% and Black only 40–41%. Above 2200 the result is almost even (47% against 45%). In master games over the board, White wins 32.5%, Black 20% and 48% are drawn.
