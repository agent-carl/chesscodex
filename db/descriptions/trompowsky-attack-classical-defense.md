The Classical Defense to the [Trompowsky Attack](/openings/trompowsky-attack) is 1.d4 Nf6 2.Bg5 e6. White's bishop move prepares to exchange on f6 and give Black doubled pawns; with 2...e6 Black avoids them, because the queen can recapture if White plays Bxf6.

### The idea

Besides keeping the pawn structure intact, 2...e6 opens a diagonal for Black's king's bishop. The drawback is that the knight on f6 is now pinned, which can be annoying. The line 3.e4 is known as the [Big Center Variation](/openings/trompowsky-attack-classical-defense-big-center-variation).

### Other replies to 2.Bg5

- 2...Ne4 is the most common reply. Black moves the same piece twice but attacks the bishop; White usually retreats with 3.Bf4 or 3.Bh4, or defends it with 3.h4, the Raptor Variation.
- 2...d5 and 2...c5 grab space in the centre and allow the doubled pawns, counting on the bishop pair.
- 2...g6 prepares a fianchetto, and 2...c6 threatens a later ...Qb6.

### History

The opening is named after the one-time Brazilian champion Octávio Trompowsky (1897–1984), who played it in the 1930s and 1940s. Karel Opočenský also played it then. Julian Hodgson, Antoaneta Stefanova and Arjun Erigaisi are among the grandmasters who often employ it, and Magnus Carlsen played it in the first game of the 2016 World Championship against Sergey Karjakin.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 49.3% of the 2.2 million games and Black 45.1%. Black is slightly ahead below 1800, while from 2200 up White leads 49.9% to 42.3%. In 5,696 master games over the board White won 33.3%, Black 30.1%, and 36.7% were drawn.
