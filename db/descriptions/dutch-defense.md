The Dutch Defense (1.d4 f5) is Black's most aggressive answer to 1.d4. By playing ...f5, Black fights for the e4-square immediately and prepares a kingside attack, creating an unbalanced position from the very first move.

### Origins

The opening is named after Elias Stein, a Dutch player (born in France, active in The Hague), who recommended 1...f5 in his 1789 book on chess. It was long considered risky. It became respectable in the 20th century, above all through world champion Mikhail Botvinnik, who used it in world championship matches. The [Leningrad Variation](/openings/dutch-defense-leningrad-variation) was developed by players from Leningrad in the 1950s. In recent years grandmasters such as Hikaru Nakamura and Magnus Carlsen have also played it.

### The idea

1...f5 controls e4 and takes space on the kingside, much like a reversed Sicilian. It fights for the initiative instead of accepting a passive position. The cost is king safety: the e8–h5 diagonal is weakened, and Black must watch out for early attacks. Black's plans depend on the setup:

- **Stonewall**: pawns on c6, d5, e6 and f5 give a rock-solid centre and a firm grip on e4, but leave a hole on e5.
- **Leningrad**: ...g6, ...Bg7 and ...d6, a King's Indian-style setup with ...e5 later.
- **Classical**: ...e6, ...Be7 and ...d6, a flexible, less committal system.

### Main lines

- [Stonewall Variation](/openings/dutch-defense-stonewall-variation) with ...d5: very solid and strategic.
- [Leningrad Variation](/openings/dutch-defense-leningrad-variation) with ...g6: the most dynamic setup.
- [Classical Variation](/openings/dutch-defense-classical-variation) with ...e6 and ...d6.

White can avoid these structures with the [Staunton Gambit](/openings/dutch-defense-staunton-gambit) 2.e4, the [Hopton Attack](/openings/dutch-defense-hopton-attack) 2.Bg5, or the [Raphael Variation](/openings/dutch-defense-raphael-variation) 2.Nc3. Black must know how to handle all of them.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Unbalanced from the first move, so Black plays for a win against 1.d4.
- Clear strategic plans: the fight for e4 and kingside expansion with ...g5 or ...f4.
- Less mainstream than the [Queen's Gambit Declined](/openings/queens-gambit-declined) or the [King's Indian](/openings/kings-indian-defense), so opponents are often less prepared.

**Weaknesses**

- Weakens Black's king, especially the e8–h5 diagonal and the e6-square.
- White has several dangerous anti-Dutch lines (Staunton Gambit, 2.Bg5, early h4 ideas).
- The Stonewall leaves a permanent hole on e5, which White can occupy with a knight.

### Who should play it

The Dutch suits ambitious players who want a fighting, unbalanced game against 1.d4 and like kingside attacks. It is effective against positional players who expect a quiet Queen's Gambit and don't know the Dutch structures. It is least comfortable against well-prepared opponents who choose an anti-Dutch system, or who calmly occupy e5 against the Stonewall.

### The numbers

On Lichess White scores 50–51% and Black 45–46% below 2200, and 50% against 43% above it. In master games over the board, White wins 36%, Black 23% and 41% are drawn. That is a fighting profile, with fewer draws than the Queen's Gambit Declined or the Slav.
