The Three Knights Game of the [Petrov Defense](/openings/petrovs-defense) (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nf6 3.Nc3) is a quiet way for White to meet the Petrov. Instead of taking on e5 or playing d4, White simply defends e4 and develops.

### The idea

White often plays 3.Nc3 to avoid the lines that are unique to the Petrov. Black's most common and best-regarded answer, 3...Nc6, transposes to the [Four Knights Game](/openings/four-knights-game). The same position can also arise from the [Vienna Game](/openings/vienna-game-falkbeer-variation) after 1.e4 e5 2.Nc3 Nf6 3.Nf3.

### Main lines

- 3...Nc6, transposing to the Four Knights Game.
- 3...Bb4, the main alternative. After 4.Nxe5 0-0 5.Be2 Re8 6.Nd3 Bxc3 7.dxc3 Nxe4 8.Nf4 d6 9.0-0, White has a slight advantage thanks to the bishop pair.
- The similar position with the black knight on c6 instead of f6 is the [Three Knights Opening](/openings/three-knights-opening), which also usually leads to the Four Knights.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- White avoids the theory of the main Petrov lines.
- Simple, natural development.

**Weaknesses**

- Black can transpose at once to the well-known Four Knights Game with 3...Nc6.

### Who should play it

The Three Knights Game suits White players who want to sidestep the Petrov and are happy to play the Four Knights Game.

### The numbers

On Lichess White's lead shrinks as ratings rise: 51.9% against 43.8% under 1400, 50.3% against 45.5% at 1400–1799, 48.7% against 46.0% at 1800–2199 and 46.4% against 45.2% from 2200 up. In about 4,900 master games 51.3% were drawn; White won 29.9% and Black 18.8%.
