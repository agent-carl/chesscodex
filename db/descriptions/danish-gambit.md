The Danish Gambit (1.e4 e5 2.d4 exd4 3.c3) is one of the most aggressive openings in chess. White offers one or even two pawns for a huge lead in development and two bishops raking across the board towards Black's king.

### Origins

The Danish player Martin Severin From played it at the Paris tournament of 1867 and is usually given credit for it. However, the Swedish player Hans Lindehn was playing 1.e4 e5 2.d4 exd4 3.c3 regularly from 1857 at the latest. In Denmark itself it is called the Nordic Gambit. It was popular with attacking masters such as Alexander Alekhine, Frank Marshall, Joseph Blackburne and Jacques Mieses, but it lost favour in the 1920s as better defences for Black were found.

### The idea

After 3...dxc3 4.Bc4 cxb2 5.Bxb2, White has given up two pawns. In return White has both bishops aimed at Black's kingside, with the c4-bishop on f7 and the b2-bishop on the long diagonal, plus a big lead in development. Black must develop quickly and give back material at the right moment.

### Main lines

- [Accepted](/openings/danish-gambit-accepted) 3...dxc3 4.Bc4 cxb2 5.Bxb2, with several defences:
  - [Schlechter Defence](/openings/danish-gambit-accepted-schlechter-defense) 5...d5: Carl Schlechter recommended returning a pawn to free Black's game. This is one of the most reliable defences, and it made the Danish much less popular.
  - [5...Nf6](/openings/danish-gambit-accepted-classical-defense) and [5...Bb4+](/openings/danish-gambit-accepted-copenhagen-defense): natural development.
- Declined: the [Sørensen Defence](/openings/danish-gambit-declined-sorensen-defense) 3...d5, or 3...Qe7.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- An enormous lead in development and two powerful bishops.
- Many traps and quick wins against unprepared opponents.
- Great for learning attacking play and the value of development.

**Weaknesses**

- With the Schlechter Defence, Black returns material and reaches a comfortable position that isn't what White wants from a gambit.
- Black can also decline with 3...d5 and get easy play.
- Rarely played in top-level chess.

### Who should play it

The Danish suits attacking players who love open positions and bishops, and it is a great club and blitz weapon. It is effective against opponents who grab both pawns and then develop slowly. It is less effective against well-prepared defenders who know 5...d5 or who decline with 3...d5.

### The numbers

On Lichess White does well at club level: below 2200 White wins 53.5–55% and Black 42–43.5%. Above 2200 it is closer, 48% against 46%. There are only about 200 master games with it, too few to judge.
