The Blackmar-Diemer Gambit (1.d4 d5 2.e4 dxe4 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.f3) is an aggressive gambit for White. White gives up a pawn to open the f-file and get rapid development and attacking chances on the kingside. It is controversial: popular with club players, but rarely seen in master chess.

### Origins

It grew out of the earlier Blackmar Gambit, named after Armand Blackmar, a little-known New Orleans player of the late 19th century. The original form 1.d4 d5 2.e4 dxe4 3.f3 proved unsound. In 1889 Ignatz von Popiel introduced 3.Nc3. The modern form owes much to the German master Emil Josef Diemer (1908–1990), who popularised 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.f3. International Master Gary Lane has advocated it for club players.

### The idea

After 4...exf3 5.Nxf3, White is a pawn down but has a lead in development, an open f-file and free lines for the bishops. White castles short, often plays Bd3, Qe1–h4 or Qe2, and attacks on the kingside, sometimes sacrificing on f7 or h7. Black has an extra pawn and a solid position, and must survive the initiative.

### Main lines

Black's five main defences after 5.Nxf3:

- [Ziegler Defence](/openings/blackmar-diemer-gambit-accepted-ziegler-defense) 5...c6: considered Black's most critical answer today.
- [Teichmann Defence](/openings/blackmar-diemer-gambit-accepted-teichmann-defense) 5...Bg4: pins the knight.
- [Euwe Defence](/openings/blackmar-diemer-gambit-accepted-euwe-defense) 5...e6: a French-style setup.
- **Bogoljubov Defence** 5...g6: fianchettoes the bishop.
- **Gunderam Defence** 5...Bf5: White's main reply is 6.Ne5.

Black can also decline, for example with the Lemberger Countergambit 3...e5 or the [Zeller Defence](/openings/blackmar-diemer-gambit-zeller-defense) 3...Bf5.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Fast development and attacking chances from the first moves.
- Many opponents answering 1.d4 with 1...d5 don't expect it.
- Clear, repeatable attacking plans.

**Weaknesses**

- Its soundness is heavily debated. Most masters dismiss it, and Andrew Martin compared playing it to "shopping for a tombstone".
- Well-prepared opponents (especially with 5...c6) keep the extra pawn and a good position.
- It only works against 1...d5; against 1...Nf6 White needs a different plan.

### Who should play it

The BDG suits attacking club players who enjoy gambits and open kingside attacks and are ready to be a pawn down. It is effective against defensive players who don't know the specific lines. It is less suitable for players who want objectively sound openings or who play against strong, well-prepared opposition.

### The numbers

On Lichess the BDG depends heavily on rating. Below 1400 Black scores better, 48.6% against 47%. From 1400 to 2200 White does better, 51–51.6% against 44.3–45.7%. Above 2200 it is 49% against 45%. There are only about 300 master games, too few to draw firm conclusions, which shows how rarely strong players use it.
