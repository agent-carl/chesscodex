The Philidor Defense (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d6) protects the e5-pawn with a pawn instead of the usual 2...Nc6. It is a solid but somewhat passive choice. Black builds a firm "strongpoint" on e5 and aims for a compact, hard-to-break position.

### Origins

The opening is named after the 18th-century French player and composer François-André Danican Philidor. He advocated 2...d6 as an alternative to 2...Nc6, and his original idea was to challenge White's centre with ...f7–f5. The most famous game with it is Paul Morphy's "Opera Game" of 1858 against the Duke of Brunswick and Count Isouard, which went 3.d4 Bg4. The Philidor had almost vanished from top-level chess by the First World War. Bent Larsen experimented with a ...g6 setup, and Étienne Bacrot and Liviu-Dieter Nisipeanu have tried it occasionally.

### The idea

With 2...d6 Black keeps e5 firmly defended and prepares a compact setup: ...Nf6, ...Nbd7, ...Be7, ...O-O and ...c6. This is the Hanham Variation idea of a strongpoint defence of e5. The drawback is that the dark-squared bishop is blocked behind the d6-pawn, and White gets more space. In sharper lines Black gives up the centre with ...exd4 for freer piece play.

### Main lines

- [Exchange Variation](/openings/philidor-defense-exchange-variation) 3.d4 exd4: the main line continues 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 Be7. Black gives up the centre for simple development.
- [Hanham Variation](/openings/philidor-defense-hanham-variation) 3...Nd7 and the [Lion setup](/openings/philidor-defense-lion-variation) with ...Nf6 and ...Nbd7: Black keeps the pawn on e5.
- Philidor Countergambit 3...f5: often considered too risky and dubious, but played by David Bronstein and Hikaru Nakamura.
- [Morphy Gambit](/openings/philidor-defense-morphy-gambit) 4.Bc4 after 3...exd4, a sharp attacking choice for White.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Solid and easy to learn: the same setup works against most White plans.
- Avoids the heavy theory of the Ruy Lopez and the Italian.
- A good choice for amateur players who want a defensive strategy.

**Weaknesses**

- Passive: Black gets less space and the f8-bishop is limited.
- White has many ways to keep a lasting edge.
- There are tactical traps, especially if Black mixes up the move order of ...Nf6, ...Nbd7 and ...Be7.

### Who should play it

The Philidor suits solid, defensive players who like a fixed setup and slow manoeuvring, and who want to avoid main-line theory against 1.e4 e5. It is effective against opponents who attack without a plan. It is less effective against positional players who simply take space and wait.

### The numbers

On Lichess White scores 51–52% and Black 43–44.5% at every band below 2200, and 50% against 43% above it. It is played very often at club level: most of its Lichess games are below 1800. In master games over the board, White wins 41%, Black 25% and 34.5% are drawn, one of the better master scores for White among the replies to 2.Nf3.
