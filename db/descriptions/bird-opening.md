Bird's Opening (1.f4) is an offbeat but respectable first move. White fights for the e5-square and plays a reversed [Dutch Defense](/openings/dutch-defense) with an extra tempo, accepting a slightly weakened kingside in exchange for attacking chances.

### Origins

The move 1.f4 was first mentioned by Luis Ramírez de Lucena in 1497. It is named after the English player Henry Bird, who first played it in 1855 and continued to use it for the next 40 years. The *Hereford Times* named it after him in 1885. In the first half of the 20th century Aron Nimzowitsch and Savielly Tartakower sometimes played it, and later grandmasters who used it with some regularity include Bent Larsen, Mikhail Gurevich and Henrik Danielsen.

### The idea

1.f4 controls e5 and prepares Nf3, e3 or g3, and often b3 and Bb2 for strong pressure along the long diagonal. The position resembles a Dutch Defence with colours reversed. White gets good attacking chances on the kingside, but the f-pawn move slightly weakens White's own king, especially the e1–h4 diagonal.

### Main lines

- [Dutch Variation](/openings/bird-opening-dutch-variation) 1...d5: the most common reply. The game resembles a Dutch with colours reversed, and White chooses between Stonewall setups (d4, e3), Leningrad-style setups (g3, Bg2) and the b3/Bb2 fianchetto.
- [From's Gambit](/openings/bird-opening-froms-gambit) 1...e5: Black's sharpest reply, sacrificing a pawn to attack White's weakened kingside. It includes [Lasker's Variation](/openings/bird-opening-froms-gambit-lasker-variation) with ...g5, introduced by Emanuel Lasker in 1892. White can avoid it with 2.e4, transposing to the [King's Gambit](/openings/kings-gambit).

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Unusual, so opponents are often less prepared.
- Clear kingside plans and good attacking chances.
- Flexible structures: Stonewall, Leningrad-style or queenside fianchetto.

**Weaknesses**

- It weakens White's kingside, and From's Gambit exploits that directly.
- Statistically it underperforms: White's score in master databases is noticeably lower than with 1.e4 or 1.d4.
- White gives up some of the first-move advantage.

### Who should play it

The Bird suits creative attacking players who like to leave theory early and enjoy Dutch-type structures. It is effective against opponents who only know how to respond to 1.e4 and 1.d4. It is less suitable for players who want to keep the full first-move advantage, and it needs a good answer to From's Gambit.

### The numbers

On Lichess the Bird is balanced. Below 1400 Black actually wins more often, 49% against 46%. From 1400 to 2200 White wins 49.5–50% and Black 46%, and above 2200 it is 47% against 46%. In master games over the board White does poorly: White wins 28%, Black 34% and 38% are drawn.
