The Catalan Opening (1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.g3, usually followed by ...d5 and Bg2) combines the [Queen's Gambit](/openings/queens-gambit) with a kingside fianchetto. White's bishop on g2 puts long-term pressure on Black's queenside, and the opening is a favourite of world champions for its safe but persistent pressure.

### Origins

The name honours Catalonia. At the 1929 Barcelona tournament, the organisers asked Savielly Tartakower to create a new variation in tribute to Catalonia's chess history. The Catalan had been played before, but it became established there. In the 1930s and 1940s world champions Capablanca, Alekhine and Botvinnik used it. Garry Kasparov and Viktor Korchnoi played it five times in their 1983 Candidates semi-final. Vladimir Kramnik used it three times in the 2006 world championship, Viswanathan Anand four times against Veselin Topalov in 2010, and Magnus Carlsen played it against Ian Nepomniachtchi in 2021.

### The idea

White builds a classical centre with d4 and c4, and adds g3 and Bg2. The g2-bishop presses along the long diagonal against d5, c6 and b7, and White's king is safe behind it. The pressure is mostly on the queenside, and even in endgames the bishop can dominate. Black's problem is usually the c8-bishop and the queenside, especially after ...dxc4.

### Main lines

- [Open Catalan](/openings/catalan-opening-open-defense) 4...dxc4: Black takes the pawn and either tries to keep it with ...a6 and ...b5, or returns it for free development. The [classical 5.Nf3 Be7](/openings/catalan-opening-open-defense-classical-line) line is one of the main tests.
- [Closed Catalan](/openings/catalan-opening-closed) with ...Be7, ...O-O, ...Nbd7 and ...c6: Black keeps the pawn on d5 and gets a very solid but somewhat cramped position, as in the [main line with Qc2 and Nbd2](/openings/catalan-opening-closed-main-line).
- 4...Bb4+ lines, where Black exchanges the dark-squared bishop or disrupts White's development.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Safe and solid for White, with long-lasting pressure and few risks.
- Trusted in world championship matches for almost a century.
- Positions are strategic, so understanding counts for more than memory.

**Weaknesses**

- Black has solid ways to equalise, and the pressure can fizzle out if White plays without a plan.
- The Open Catalan needs concrete knowledge about how to win back the c4-pawn.
- It is slow: players who want quick attacks may find it dull.

### Who should play it

The Catalan suits positional 1.d4 players who like long-term pressure, endgames and piece play, and who want an opening that also works at the top. It is effective against players who defend passively or don't know how to free the c8-bishop. It is less effective against well-prepared opponents who know the Open Catalan and give the pawn back at the right time.

### The numbers

On Lichess White scores 52–53% and Black 41.5–43.5% below 2200, and 50% against 41% above it, one of the better results for White among the 1.d4 openings on this site. In master games over the board, the Catalan is very solid: 52.5% of games are drawn, White wins 27% and Black 20%.
