The Queen's Gambit Declined (1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6) is Black's classical, rock-solid answer to the [Queen's Gambit](/openings/queens-gambit). Black refuses the c4-pawn and keeps a firm pawn on d5, accepting a temporarily passive light-squared bishop in exchange for a very sound position.

### Origins

The QGD was the main battleground of classical chess from the late 19th century onwards. It was the centre of the 1927 world championship match between José Raúl Capablanca and Alexander Alekhine, in which almost every game began this way. Among its many named lines, the [Tarrasch Defense](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-tarrasch-defense) comes from Siegbert Tarrasch and the [Ragozin Defense](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-ragozin-defense) from the Soviet master Viacheslav Ragozin. Modern champions from Karpov to Carlsen have relied on it.

### The idea

With 2...e6 Black supports d5 and prepares ...Nf6, ...Be7 and castling. The problem is the c8-bishop, which is shut in behind the e6-pawn. Much of Black's play is about freeing it, with ...b6 and ...Bb7, with ...dxc4 and ...c5, or with ...e5. White wants to exploit the small space advantage. A famous plan is the minority attack in the Exchange Variation, where White advances b4–b5 to create a weakness on c6.

### Main lines

- [Orthodox Defense](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-orthodox-defense): 4.Bg5 Be7 5.e3 O-O 6.Nf3 Nbd7, the classic main line of the [Modern Variation](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-modern-variation).
- [Exchange Variation](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-exchange-variation) cxd5 exd5: the "Carlsbad structure" and the minority attack.
- [Harrwitz Attack](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-harrwitz-attack) 4.Bf4 (5.Bf4 in some move orders): a modern, flexible alternative to Bg5.
- [Ragozin Defense](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-ragozin-defense) ...Bb4: a more active setup, related to the Nimzo-Indian.
- [Tarrasch Defense](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-tarrasch-defense) 3...c5: Black accepts an isolated d-pawn for free piece play. The [Semi-Tarrasch](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-semi-tarrasch-defense) avoids the isolated pawn.
- Counter-attacks after 2.c4: the [Albin Countergambit](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-albin-countergambit) 2...e5 and the [Chigorin Defense](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-chigorin-defense) 2...Nc6. Both are strictly speaking separate openings.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- One of the most solid defences in chess. Black rarely gets into immediate trouble.
- Clear, well-understood plans that teach classical strategy.
- Trusted at the highest level for more than a century.

**Weaknesses**

- Black is passive for a while, and winning chances are limited against a careful opponent.
- The c8-bishop can remain a problem if Black misses the right freeing break.
- It needs positional patience; players who want early tactics find it dull.

### Who should play it

The QGD suits positional players who want a reliable defence against 1.d4 and are happy to wait for chances. It is good against aggressive players who overpress, because Black's position has very few weaknesses. It is less suited to players who must win with Black, since a well-prepared opponent can steer into the drawish Exchange lines. For a more active alternative with the same first moves, consider the [Slav](/openings/slav-defense) or the [Nimzo-Indian](/openings/nimzo-indian-defense).

### The numbers

On Lichess White scores 51–52% below 2200 and Black 44%. Above 2200 it is 49% against 42%. In master games over the board, draws dominate at 47%, White wins 33% and Black 19%. The line is hard to beat but gives Black few wins.
