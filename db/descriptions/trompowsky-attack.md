The Trompowsky Attack (1.d4 Nf6 2.Bg5) is a direct way to sidestep the huge theory of the Indian defences. White develops the bishop at once and often exchanges it for Black's knight, giving Black doubled pawns, or keeps it active and plays for a quick e4.

### Origins

The opening is named after the Brazilian master Octávio Trompowsky (1897–1984), who played it in the 1930s and 1940s. The Czech master Karel Opočenský developed it at the same time, so it is also known as the Opočenský Opening, and William Allen Ruth worked on it independently in Pennsylvania. Julian Hodgson, Antoaneta Stefanova and Arjun Erigaisi are among the grandmasters who play it often. Magnus Carlsen used it in the first game of the 2016 World Championship.

### The idea

2.Bg5 threatens to take on f6 and double Black's pawns. If Black allows it, White gets a better structure in return for the bishop pair. White also avoids the whole theory of the King's Indian, Nimzo-Indian, Queen's Indian and Grünfeld. Typical follow-ups are e3, c3, Nd2 or Nf3 and sometimes f3 and e4 to build a big centre.

### Main lines

- 2...Ne4: the most common reply, attacking the bishop. White can retreat with [3.Bh4](/openings/trompowsky-attack-edge-variation) or 3.Bf4, or play the sharp [3.h4](/openings/trompowsky-attack-raptor-variation), the Raptor Variation.
- [2...e6](/openings/trompowsky-attack-classical-defense): Black avoids the doubled pawns, since after Bxf6 the queen recaptures. White often continues with [3.e4](/openings/trompowsky-attack-classical-defense-big-center-variation).
- 2...d5: Black allows the doubled pawns in return for the bishop pair.
- 2...c5: Black grabs central space and counterattacks, for example in the [Poisoned Pawn line](/openings/trompowsky-attack-poisoned-pawn-variation) 3.d5 Qb6.
- 2...g6: a fianchetto setup.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Avoids a huge amount of opening theory.
- Creates imbalances early: bishop against knight, doubled pawns.
- Many Black players are less familiar with it than with the main lines.

**Weaknesses**

- White gives up the bishop pair in many lines, which can matter in the long run.
- Black has several good replies, and 2...Ne4 and 2...c5 lead to sharp play that White must know.
- It gives no clear advantage against accurate play.

### Who should play it

The Trompowsky suits practical 1.d4 players who want to avoid main-line theory and like unbalanced positions. It is effective against Indian-defence players who rely on their preparation after 2.c4. It is less effective against opponents who know the sharp 2...Ne4 and 2...c5 lines well.

### The numbers

On Lichess the Trompowsky is even below 1800, with about 48% each. From 1800 up White does better, with 49.5% against 43–45%. In master games over the board, White wins 34%, Black 28.5% and 37.5% are drawn.
