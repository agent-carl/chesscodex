The Polerio Defense is 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Nf6 4.Ng5 d5 5.exd5 Na5, Black's standard reply to the [Knight Attack](/openings/italian-game-two-knights-defense-knight-attack) in the [Two Knights Defense](/openings/italian-game-two-knights-defense). It has also been called the Chigorin Gambit. The knight attacks the bishop on c4 before White can move the d5-pawn and renew the pressure on f7.

### The idea

Black gives up a pawn for a lead in development. In the 19th century Mikhail Chigorin demonstrated Black's compensation in the line 6.Bb5+ c6 7.dxc6 bxc6, which is now the main line.

### Main lines

After 6.Bb5+ c6 7.dxc6 bxc6 White has three common moves:

- 8.Be2 h6 9.Nf3 e4 10.Ne5 was the main line from the 19th century. White is behind in development but has an extra pawn and the better structure, while Black has two isolated pawns. 9.Nh3, popularised by Wilhelm Steinitz and revived by Bobby Fischer in the 1960s and Nigel Short in the 1990s, is thought to be about as good.
- 8.Bd3 was still considered a mistake by Jan Pinski in 2004 but is now the main line of the whole 4.Ng5 variation.
- [8.Qf3](/openings/italian-game-two-knights-defense-polerio-defense-bogoljubow-variation), the Bogoljubow Variation, pins the c6-pawn to the rook on a8. Black can sacrifice the rook with 8...cxb5, as Joseph Henry Blackburne did, but 8...Be7 and 8...Rb8 are more common.

8.Ba4? is a notable mistake: after 8...h6 9.Nf3 e4 the knight must retreat to g1 or go to e5, where 10...Qd4 wins material.

Black can also answer 6.Bb5+ with 6...Bd7. White's other sixth move is [6.d3](/openings/italian-game-two-knights-defense-polerio-defense-kieseritzky-variation), the Kieseritzky or Morphy Variation, which tries to keep the pawn. It is relatively unpopular, since after 6...h6 7.Nf3 e4 8.Qe2 Nxc4 9.dxc4 Bc5 Black gets good chances for the pawn and White gives up the bishop pair.

### The numbers

On Lichess Black wins 48.6% of the 4.5 million games and White 47.5%. Black does best under 1400, 51.9% against 45.1%; from 2200 up White is ahead, 48.3% against 46.1%. In 2,858 master games over the board White won 31.1%, Black 23.5%, and 45.3% were drawn.
