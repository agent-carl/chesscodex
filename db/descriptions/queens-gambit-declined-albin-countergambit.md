The Albin Countergambit (1.d4 d5 2.c4 e5) is an uncommon and aggressive answer to the [Queen's Gambit](/openings/queens-gambit). After the usual 3.dxe5 d4 Black has given up a pawn for a wedge in the centre on d4 and some attacking chances.

### Origins

The opening was first played by Cavallotti against Salvioli at Milan in 1881, but it takes its name from Adolf Albin, who played it against Emanuel Lasker at New York in 1893. It is seldom seen at master level, though Alexander Morozevich used it successfully in the 2000s.

### The idea

The pawn on d4 is stronger than it may appear, and it gives Black a wedge in the centre. White often tries to return the extra pawn at a good moment to gain a positional advantage.

### Main lines

- 4.Nf3, the [Normal Line](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-albin-countergambit-normal-line). The main line continues 4...Nc6 (4...c5, the [Tartakower Defense](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-albin-countergambit-tartakower-defense), allows 5.e3 because Black no longer has the bishop check). White's main options are then 5.a3, 5.Nbd2 ([Modern Line](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-albin-countergambit-modern-line)) and 5.g3 ([Fianchetto Variation](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-albin-countergambit-fianchetto-variation)), perhaps White's surest try for an advantage, with Bg2 and Nbd2; Black often castles queenside, as in 5.g3 Be6 6.Nbd2 Qd7 7.Bg2 O-O-O 8.O-O Bh3.
- 4.e3?, which walks into the [Lasker Trap](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-albin-countergambit-lasker-trap): 4...Bb4+ 5.Bd2 dxe3! 6.Bxb4?? exf2+ 7.Ke2 fxg1=N+! 8.Rxg1 Bg4+, winning the queen, a rare underpromotion in practical play.
- 4.e4, the [Spassky Variation](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-albin-countergambit-spassky-variation), avoiding the trap; according to Minev, 4...Nc6! gives Black the better game.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- An active, surprising answer to the Queen's Gambit, with traps such as the Lasker Trap.
- A central wedge on d4 and chances for an attack.

**Weaknesses**

- It is a real gambit: White keeps an extra pawn and can return it for a positional edge.
- With careful development, especially 5.g3, White has good chances for an advantage.

### Who should play it

The Albin suits Black players who want sharp, attacking play against 1.d4 and are willing to give a pawn for it.

### The numbers

On Lichess White leads at every level: 52.3% against 43.7% under 1400, 49.7% against 46.2% at 1400–1799, 49.1% against 45.8% at 1800–2199 and 48.9% against 44.2% from 2200 up. There are only about 1,500 master games, too few to draw firm conclusions.
