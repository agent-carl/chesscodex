The Van Geet Opening (1.Nc3), also called the Dunst Opening, may have more names than any other opening: Heinrichsen, Baltic, Sleipnir, Queen's Knight Attack, Millard's Opening and, in German, der Linksspringer, among others. It is an irregular opening classified under A00 and only the eighth most popular of the twenty possible first moves.

### Origins

The names Heinrichsen and Baltic come from the Lithuanian player Arved Heinrichsen (1879–1900). The New York master Ted Dunst (1907–1985) analysed and played it, giving it its usual American name, while the Dutch International Master and correspondence grandmaster Dick van Geet (1932–2012) played it so often that it is named after him in the Netherlands. Hugh Myers called it Millard's Opening after Henry Millard, a blind correspondence player who drew with it against Joseph Henry Blackburne in a simultaneous exhibition; Blackburne later played it himself at London 1883. Some very strong correspondence players use it often.

### The idea

1.Nc3 develops the knight to a good square where it controls e4 and d5. Its drawbacks are that it does not stop Black from occupying the centre with 1...d5, that it blocks White's c-pawn, and that after 1...d5 the knight can be hit with ...d4.

### Main lines

- 1...d5, one of Black's best replies:
  - 2.e4, White's most common answer, the same position as 1.e4 d5 2.Nc3. 2...d4 3.Nce2 followed by Ng3 is the Van Geet Attack, which Harald Keilhack calls "the core of the 1.Nc3 opening"; 2...e6, 2...c6 and 2...Nf6 transpose to the French, Caro-Kann and [Alekhine](/openings/alekhine-defense-scandinavian-variation) defences, and 2...dxe4 leads to open play;
  - 2.d4, transposing to a Queen's Pawn Game where White can play a [Jobava London](/openings/rapport-jobava-system) with Bf4;
  - 2.Nf3 and 2.e3 are also possible.
- 1...e5, the [Reversed Nimzowitsch](/openings/van-geet-opening-reversed-nimzowitsch).
- 1...c5 and 1...Nf6 are other common replies.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Develops a piece to a good square without weakening White's position.
- Quite playable, though rarely seen, and full of transpositions.

**Weaknesses**

- Does not prevent ...d5, and the knight can be harassed with ...d4.
- Blocks the c-pawn, so c3 or c4 needs a knight move first.

### Who should play it

1.Nc3 suits players who like independent, offbeat positions such as the Van Geet Attack, and who are comfortable transposing to the French, Caro-Kann or Queen's Pawn structures when Black chooses them.

### The numbers

On Lichess Black wins more often below 1800: 48.9% against 46.2% under 1400 and 48.3% against 47.5% at 1400–1799. From 1800 White leads, 50.4% against 44.9% at 1800–2199 and 49.4% against 43.8% from 2200 up. In about 4,000 master games White won 28.2%, Black 31.0%, and 40.7% were drawn.
