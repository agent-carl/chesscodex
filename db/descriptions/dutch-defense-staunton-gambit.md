The Staunton Gambit is 1.d4 f5 2.e4: against the [Dutch Defense](/openings/dutch-defense) White gives up a pawn for quick development and an attack on Black's king, which 1...f5 has already weakened a little. Black can decline with 2...d6, transposing to the Balogh Defense, but taking the pawn with 2...fxe4 is considered stronger. Its ECO codes are A82 and A83.

### Origins

The main line, 2...fxe4 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Bg5, was first played by Howard Staunton against Bernhard Horwitz in London in 1846. The gambit was once a feared weapon for White, but defensive techniques have since been found to neutralise it, and it is rarely played today: White scores only about 50 percent with it.

### Main lines

After [2...fxe4](/openings/dutch-defense-staunton-gambit-accepted) play usually continues 3.Nc3 Nf6.

- [4.Bg5](/openings/dutch-defense-staunton-gambit-2), the main line. Here 4...d5? is a common trap: 5.Bxf6 exf6 6.Qh5+ g6 7.Qxd5 Qxd5 8.Nxd5, and the knight attacks both c7 and f6, so White comes out a pawn ahead. Instead Black usually develops quickly and protects the kingside, giving back the pawn if needed, with 4...Nc6 5.d5 Ne5 6.Qd4 Nf7. 6.Qe2 is a modern alternative. White can win the pawn back with 5.Bxf6 exf6 6.Nxe4, but after 6...Qe7 the knight is hard to defend, and Black comes out with an extra pawn and the two bishops.
- 4.f3, in the style of the [Blackmar-Diemer Gambit](/openings/blackmar-diemer-gambit). After 4...exf3 White gets good compensation, so Black generally plays 4...d5 5.fxe4 dxe4. Black can also return the pawn with 4...e3 to slow White's development.
- 4.g4, the Bayonet Attack or Tartakower Variation, does not give enough compensation after 4...h6.

### Avoiding it

Black can sidestep the Staunton Gambit by playing 1...e6 before ...f5. The drawback of that move order is that White can answer 2.e4 and reach the [French Defense](/openings/french-defense), so Black no longer gets the Dutch.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 55.0% of the 940,000 games and Black 40.3%. Under 1400 it is close, 50.1% against 45.8%, while between 1800 and 2199 White wins 55.9% against 39.3%. From 2200 up the gap narrows to 51.1% against 42.5%. In 905 master games over the board White and Black each won 33.5%, and 33.0% were drawn.
