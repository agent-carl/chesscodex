The Open Defence of the [Ruy Lopez](/openings/ruy-lopez), 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 a6 4.Ba4 Nf6 5.O-O Nxe4, takes the e4-pawn: Black tries to use the time White needs to regain it to establish a foothold in the centre. Its ECO codes are C80–C83.

### The idea

Play usually continues 6.d4 b5 7.Bb3 d5 8.dxe5 Be6 ([main line](/openings/ruy-lopez-open-main-line)). 8.dxe5 attacks the d5-pawn with two pieces, all but forcing 8...Be6, the only way both to defend the pawn and to develop a new piece. White then has a variety of ninth moves, including 9.c3, 9.Be3, 9.Qe2 and 9.Nbd2.

### Main lines

- 9.c3 (the Classical Variation): Black chooses between the traditional 9...Be7 (the [Classical Defense](/openings/ruy-lopez-open-classical-defense) in this database) and the more aggressive 9...Bc5 (the [Italian Variation](/openings/ruy-lopez-open-italian-variation)), after which 10.Nbd2 O-O 11.Bc2 is White's most common line. Black must meet the attack on e4: 11...f5 and 11...Bf5 aim to keep the strongpoint on e4, or Black can play the forcing 11...Nxf2, the [Dilworth Variation](/openings/ruy-lopez-open-dilworth-variation), introduced by the English amateur Vernon Dilworth. After 12.Rxf2 f6 13.exf6 Bxf2+ 14.Kxf2 Qxf6 it has scored well for Black, with many traps for an ill-prepared opponent; the main line leads to unbalanced endgames with a strong drawing tendency. Artur Yusupov is one of the few grandmasters who has adopted the Dilworth repeatedly.
- 9.Nbd2, the [Bernstein Variation](/openings/ruy-lopez-open-bernstein-variation), was Anatoly Karpov's preferred move. In his 1978 World Championship match against Viktor Korchnoi, after 9...Nc5 10.c3 d4, Karpov introduced the surprising 11.Ng5!?, suggested by his trainer Igor Zaitsev; if 11...Qxg5, White regains the material with 12.Qf3. The line played a decisive role in the 1995 World Championship match between Garry Kasparov and Viswanathan Anand, when Anand was unable to defend it successfully as Black.
- 9.Qe2, the [Howell Attack](/openings/ruy-lopez-open-howell-attack), aims for play against d5 after Rd1. Paul Keres played it against Max Euwe and Samuel Reshevsky at the 1948 World Championship tournament.
- 6...exd4, the [Riga Variation](/openings/ruy-lopez-open-riga-variation), is considered inferior: its main line leads to an endgame that is considered to favour White, as in the famous game Capablanca–Edward Lasker, New York 1915.

### Famous games

After 9.c3 Bc5 10.Nbd2 O-O 11.Bc2 f5 12.Nb3 Bb6 13.Nfd4 Nxd4 14.Nxd4 Bxd4, the sharp La Grande Variation, 15.cxd4 f4, has unclear consequences. Perhaps the most famous game in it is Smyslov–Reshevsky from the 1945 USSR–USA radio match. An analysis of the line had just been published in a Russian chess magazine; Smyslov followed it and quickly obtained a winning position, while Reshevsky, who had not seen it, struggled in vain to solve the position over the board.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Black gets active piece play and a foothold in the centre.
- The Dilworth has scored well for Black and sets many traps.

**Weaknesses**

- The d5-pawn often needs defending.
- Some lines, such as the Riga Variation, are considered inferior.

### Who should play it

The Open Defence suits Black players who prefer active piece play and concrete lines to the slow manoeuvring of the [Closed Defence](/openings/ruy-lopez-closed).

### The numbers

In 462,225 rated Lichess games between players rated 1600 and 2500, White won 46.9%, Black 46.2%, and 7.0% were drawn. The results change sharply with rating: White wins 59.7% against 37.3% under 1400 and 57.2% against 38.9% at 1400–1799, while Black is ahead at 1800–2199, 47.7% against 45.6%, and from 2200 up, 46.7% against 43.5%. In about 7,700 master games 55.5% were drawn, White won 28.6% and Black 15.9%.
