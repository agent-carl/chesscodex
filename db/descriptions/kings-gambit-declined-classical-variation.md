The Classical Variation is 1.e4 e5 2.f4 Bc5, a common way to decline the [King's Gambit](/openings/kings-gambit). Instead of taking the pawn, Black develops the bishop to c5, where it stops White from castling kingside.

### The idea

The bishop on c5 is such a nuisance that White often spends two tempi removing it, with Nc3 and Na4 to exchange it on c5 or b6. After that White can castle without worry.

The usual continuation is 3.Nf3 d6, and then White commonly plays 4.c3, intending 5.d4, or 4.Bc4 or 4.Nc3. The variation often arises by transposition from the [Vienna Game](/openings/vienna-game) or the [Bishop's Opening](/openings/bishops-opening) when White plays f2-f4 before Nf3.

### A trap for beginners

Taking the pawn at once is a mistake: after 3.fxe5? Qh4+ White loses a rook to 4.g3 Qxe4+, which forks king and rook, or is mated after 4.Ke2 Qxe4#.

### The Rotlewi Countergambit

A rarely seen try is 3.Nf3 d6 4.b4. As in the [Evans Gambit](/openings/italian-game-evans-gambit), White gives a pawn to build a strong centre, for example 4...Bxb4 5.c3 Bc5 6.fxe5 dxe5 7.d4. It is considered slightly dubious.

### The numbers

On Lichess the results are nearly level: Black wins 48.4% of the 1.9 million games and White 48.1%. Under 1400 White leads 52.0% to 45.3%; from 2200 up Black does better, 49.7% against 44.8%. In 518 master games over the board White won 34.9%, Black 35.1%, and 29.9% were drawn.
