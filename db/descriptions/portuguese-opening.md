The Portuguese Opening (1.e4 e5 2.Bb5) is an uncommon opening in which White brings the king's bishop to b5 before developing the king's knight. Compared with the [Ruy Lopez](/openings/ruy-lopez) (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5), White delays Nf3: the f-pawn stays free to move and f2–f4 remains possible, but e5 is not under pressure, which leaves Black with a freer hand.

### The idea

White develops the bishop at once and keeps f2–f4 in reserve, at the cost of leaving e5 alone. Graham Burgess remarks that it looks like a Ruy Lopez in which White has forgotten to play 2.Nf3. Even so, the Portuguese is not as bad as it first appears, and Black should proceed carefully.

### Main lines

- 2...c6, the most common reply on Lichess, kicks the bishop at once. The game might continue 3.Ba4 Nf6, and now White can play 4.Nc3 or 4.Qe2.
- 2...Nf6 allows White to try a gambit with 3.d4, the [Portuguese Gambit](/openings/portuguese-opening-portuguese-gambit).
- 2...Nc6 lets White transpose into the Ruy Lopez with 3.Nf3.
- 2...Bc5 3.b4 is the [Miguel Gambit](/openings/portuguese-opening-miguel-gambit).

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- The f-pawn is not blocked by a knight on f3, so f2–f4 stays possible.

**Weaknesses**

- There is no pressure on e5, which gives Black a freer hand.
- Black can kick the bishop straight away with 2...c6.

### The numbers

On Lichess it is rare: it appears in fewer than one in a thousand games that start 1.e4 e5. Its results change with the rating. Under 1400 Black wins 57.2% and White 37.1%, and at 1400–1799 Black still leads, 51.7% against 44.4%. At 1800–2199 it is nearly level, 48.2% for Black against 47.7% for White, and from 2200 up White is ahead, 49.0% against 45.1%. There are only 62 master games, too few to draw conclusions. Stockfish rates the position after 2.Bb5 as about equal (−0.09).
