The Nimzo-Indian Defense (1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Bb4) is considered one of the best defences to 1.d4. By pinning the knight on c3, Black stops White from playing e4 easily, and is ready to give up the bishop pair to give White doubled c-pawns.

### Origins

The opening is named after Aron Nimzowitsch, one of the founders of the hypermodern school, who developed it in the 1910s and 1920s. Hypermodern players believed the centre could be controlled by pieces from a distance instead of occupied by pawns, and the Nimzo-Indian is the most successful product of that idea. It has been played by nearly every world champion since Capablanca, including Botvinnik, Spassky, Karpov, Kasparov and Carlsen.

### The idea

3...Bb4 controls e4 without a pawn. Black is often happy to play ...Bxc3 and fight against White's bishop pair. In return, White gets doubled c-pawns, and Black blockades them with ...c5, ...d6 and ...e5, or attacks them with ...Ba6 and ...Nc6–a5. White's aims are to keep the bishops, avoid the doubled pawns (with Qc2) or build a big centre with f3 and e4.

### Main lines

- [Classical Variation](/openings/nimzo-indian-defense-classical-variation) 4.Qc2: White recaptures on c3 with the queen, keeping the pawn structure intact, at the cost of time.
- [Rubinstein System](/openings/nimzo-indian-defense-rubinstein-system) 4.e3: flexible development, leading to the [Normal Variation](/openings/nimzo-indian-defense-normal-variation) 4...O-O and the [Gligoric System](/openings/nimzo-indian-defense-normal-variation-gligoric-system), one of the richest structures in the opening.
- [Sämisch Variation](/openings/nimzo-indian-defense-samisch-variation) 4.a3: White forces the doubled pawns at once and plays for f3 and e4.
- [Leningrad Variation](/openings/nimzo-indian-defense-leningrad-variation) 4.Bg5 and the [Kmoch Variation](/openings/nimzo-indian-defense-kmoch-variation) 4.f3: sharper tries.
- [St. Petersburg Variation](/openings/nimzo-indian-defense-st-petersburg-variation) 4.e3 b6: Black adds a queenside fianchetto.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Very sound and flexible: Black can choose solid or sharp setups.
- Creates an imbalance (bishops against knights, doubled pawns) from move four, so Black can play for a win.
- Rich in instructive strategy about pawn structure and blockade.

**Weaknesses**

- Only possible after 3.Nc3. Many White players go 3.Nf3 or 3.g3 to avoid it, so Black also needs the [Queen's Indian](/openings/queens-indian-defense), the [Bogo-Indian](/openings/bogo-indian-defense) or an answer to the [Catalan](/openings/catalan-opening).
- Giving up the dark-squared bishop requires understanding when the doubled pawns are really weak.
- Positions can be strategically complex, with long manoeuvring phases.

### Who should play it

The Nimzo suits strategic players who like concrete imbalances and structures, and it rewards understanding more than memory. It is excellent against White players who rely on a big pawn centre, because the pin undermines it from move three. It is less effective against flexible move orders that avoid Nc3 altogether.

### The numbers

On Lichess White scores 48–50% and Black 46–47% across all bands. Above 2200 it is 48% against 44%. In master games over the board the Nimzo is one of Black's best defences to 1.d4 in this database: White wins 29%, Black 24% and 47% are drawn.
