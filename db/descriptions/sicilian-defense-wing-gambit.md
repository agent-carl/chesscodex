The Wing Gambit is 1.e4 c5 2.b4: White offers the b-pawn to deflect Black's c-pawn and win back control of the central square d4. For the pawn White gets quicker development and a central advantage, but it is not generally considered one of White's better choices against the [Sicilian](/openings/sicilian-defense). It is virtually never seen at professional level and is more common among amateurs.

### Main lines

- After 2...cxb4 the usual continuation is [3.a3](/openings/sicilian-defense-wing-gambit-marshall-variation). Black can take again with [3...bxa3](/openings/sicilian-defense-wing-gambit-carlsbad-variation), and then the main line is 4.Nxa3, with 4.Bxa3 and 4.d4 also seen.
- 3...d5 is now considered stronger than 3...bxa3. Here White must avoid 4.exd5 Qxd5 5.axb4, which loses the rook to 5...Qe5+, a blunder actually seen in tournament play, Shirazi–Peters, Berkeley 1984. 5.Nf3 is better.
- 3.d4 is another popular third move. George Koltanowski, David Bronstein and World Champion Alexander Alekhine have played it.
- Black can also decline, or at least delay, the gambit with 2...d5.

### Delayed versions

White can postpone the gambit by a move with 2.Nf3 and 3.b4. This [Wing Gambit Deferred](/openings/sicilian-defense-wing-gambit-deferred) is considered best when Black has answered 2...e6. After 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.b4, the [Portsmouth Gambit](/openings/sicilian-defense-portsmouth-gambit), Black is reluctant to decline because of the threat b5, which would drive the knight away. White can also prepare b4 with 2.a3.

The name Wing Gambit is also used for other early b4 gambits, for example 1.e4 e5 2.Bc4 Bc5 3.b4 in the Bishop's Opening, which Alexander McDonnell introduced in his 1834 match against La Bourdonnais.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 49.0% of the 4.1 million games and Black 47.1%. Under 1400 Black is ahead, 49.8% against 47.3%, and from 2200 up the sides are level, 47.4% against 47.2%. In 688 master games over the board Black won 40.8%, White 27.2%, and 32.0% were drawn.
