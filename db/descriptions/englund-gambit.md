The Englund Gambit (1.d4 e5) is an aggressive but dubious reply to 1.d4. Black gives up the e-pawn at once, hoping for quick development and tactical traps. It is almost never seen in top-level play, but it is very popular in fast online games.

### Origins

1.d4 e5 was originally called the Charlick Gambit, after Henry Charlick (1845–1916), the Australian champion who introduced 2...d6 in the early 1890s. The main line with 2...Nc6 and 3...Qe7 was developed by Kārlis Bētiņš (1867–1943). The Swedish player Fritz Englund (1871–1933) sponsored a thematic tournament in 1932–33 in which all games had to start from this gambit, and the opening was named after him. Paul Keres tried it at top level, and Henri Grob used the 3...Qe7 line often. In online blitz, Kamran Shirazi has played it more than 1,400 times on Lichess.

### The idea

After 2.dxe5 Nc6 3.Nf3 Qe7, Black attacks the e5-pawn and sets traps along the e7–b4 diagonal. The best-known trap: 4.Bf4 Qb4+ 5.Bd2 Qxb2 6.Bc3? Bb4! 7.Qd2 Bxc3 8.Qxc3 Qc1#. White must avoid 6.Bc3 and keep things simple.

### Main lines

- [Main line](/openings/englund-gambit-main-line) 2.dxe5 Nc6 3.Nf3 Qe7: the classic version with the trap above.
- [Soller Gambit](/openings/englund-gambit-soller-gambit) 2...f6: Black offers another pawn with ...f6 for open lines.
- [Hartlaub-Charlick Gambit](/openings/englund-gambit-hartlaub-charlick-gambit) 2...d6: Charlick's original idea.
- The Zilbermints Gambit 3...Nge7, planning ...Ng6 to win back the pawn.
- White can also decline, for example with [2.d5](/openings/englund-gambit-declined) or [2.Nf3](/openings/englund-gambit-declined-reversed-alekhine).

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Many traps that catch unprepared opponents in fast games.
- It takes White out of normal 1.d4 theory at once.
- Simple, active development for Black.

**Weaknesses**

- Objectively weak: the gambit is considered unsound, and Boris Avrukh called 1...e5 "the worst possible reply" to 1.d4.
- With careful play, White keeps the better position.
- Almost never played at master level.

### Who should play it

The Englund is a surprise weapon for blitz and bullet players who enjoy traps and don't mind being objectively worse. It works against opponents who grab material and fall for the Qb4+ tricks. In serious games, a sound defence like the [Queen's Gambit Declined](/openings/queens-gambit-declined) or the [King's Indian](/openings/kings-indian-defense) is a better choice.

### The numbers

On Lichess the Englund holds up surprisingly well at club level: White scores 48.5–50% and Black 46–47.5% below 2200. Above 2200 White pulls ahead, 50.7% against 43.5%. There are only about a hundred master games with it, too few to judge, which confirms how rarely strong players use it.
