The Averbakh Variation is 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6 3.Nc3 Bg7 4.e4 d6 5.Be2 O-O 6.Bg5, a line of the [King's Indian Defense](/openings/kings-indian-defense) named after Yuri Averbakh. The bishop on g5 stops Black's usual ...e5 for the moment.

### The idea

If Black plays 6...e5 at once, White wins material: 7.dxe5 dxe5 8.Qxd8 Rxd8 9.Nd5 Nxd5 10.Bxd8 Nb6 11.Bxc7, and White is an exchange and a pawn up in an ending that should be winning.

White has several ways to continue developing, such as Qd2, Nf3, f4 or even h4. Black gets good play against all of these set-ups.

### Main lines

- 6...h6 drives the bishop back and gives Black the option of a later ...g5, though in practice this weakens Black's position.
- 6...c5 is the old main line.
- 6...Nbd7 and 6...Na6 are also played. 6...Na6 was Judit Polgár's move.

The Averbakh System of the [Modern Defense](/openings/modern-defense-averbakh-system), 1.d4 g6 2.c4 Bg7 3.Nc3 d6 4.e4, can transpose into this variation. With 5.Bg5 before Be2, White plays the [Accelerated Averbakh](/openings/kings-indian-defense-accelerated-averbakh-variation).

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 52.4% of the 420,000 games and Black 41.9%. White leads at every rating: 53.4% against 41.6% between 1800 and 2199, and 50.7% against 42.1% from 2200 up. In 6,212 master games over the board White won 39.6%, Black 24.0%, and 36.5% were drawn.
