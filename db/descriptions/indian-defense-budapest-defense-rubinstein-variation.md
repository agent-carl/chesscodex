The Rubinstein Variation of the [Budapest Gambit](/openings/indian-defense-budapest-defense) is 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.Bf4. It is named after the famous game Rubinstein–Vidmar, Berlin 1918, in which 4.Bf4 was first played. Various authors consider it the most dangerous move for Black. The bishop defends e5, and if Black answers ...Bc5, White can play e3 without blocking the bishop in, unlike in the [4.Nf3 line](/openings/indian-defense-budapest-defense-adler-variation).

### The main line

Both sides develop around the e5-pawn: 4...Nc6 5.Nf3 Bb4+. White now has an important choice between two very different moves.

- 6.Nc3 accepts the break-up of the queenside pawns after ...Bxc3+ in return for an extra pawn, the bishop pair and active play in the centre.
- 6.Nbd2 gives the pawn back but keeps a healthy pawn structure and the bishop pair. After 6...Qe7 White generally plays 7.a3, forcing the exchange on d2 and gaining space and chances for a minority attack on the queenside. 7.e3 tries to win a tempo. The maverick gambit 6...f6, which Rubinstein attributed to Ernst Grünfeld, also exists.

### 4...g5

The sideline 4...g5, invented by István Abonyi, was not well regarded until the end of the 20th century, as it weakens the f5- and h5-squares. In the 21st century Shakhriyar Mamedyarov has made successful efforts to rehabilitate it.

### The 1918 game

At Berlin 1918 Milan Vidmar, at a loss for what to play as Black against Akiba Rubinstein, took his friend Abonyi's advice to try the Budapest Gambit and won in just 24 moves. Vidmar went on to win the tournament, and after this game the gambit began to be taken seriously.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 53.3% of the 861,828 games and Black 40.8%. Masters' results favour White: in 1,351 games over the board White won 43.4%, Black 16.3%, and 40.3% were drawn.
