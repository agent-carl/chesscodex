The Göring Gambit is 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.d4 exd4 4.c3: in the [Scotch Game](/openings/scotch-game) White offers a second pawn instead of recapturing on d4. It is a delayed version of the [Danish Gambit](/openings/danish-gambit) (1.e4 e5 2.d4 exd4 3.c3), into which it can transpose. White gives up one or two pawns for a lead in development.

### Origins

Howard Staunton was the first to play it at a high level, in the 1840s, and the earliest game with it was probably played in 1843. The first game with the gambit accepted may be Meek–Morphy, New York 1857. Carl Theodor Göring brought it into master play in 1872. Alexander Alekhine, Frank Marshall, David Bronstein, Ljubomir Ljubojević and Jonathan Penrose have all played it.

### The idea

White usually puts pressure on f7 with Bc4 and Qb3, sometimes adding Ng5, and the knight jump Nc3–d5 is another common motif.

### Main lines

- 4...dxc3 5.Nxc3: White gives only one pawn. Black's most critical reply is generally considered to be [5...Bb4](/openings/scotch-game-goring-gambit-main-line): after 6.Bc4 d6 7.O-O Bxc3 8.bxc3 Nf6 White does not get enough compensation. Black's main alternative, 5...d6, usually leads to complications and roughly equal chances.
- 4...dxc3 [5.Bc4](/openings/scotch-game-goring-gambit-double-pawn-sacrifice): White offers a second pawn. After 5...cxb2 6.Bxb2 Black, unlike in the Danish proper, cannot safely answer 6...d5, and play often continues 6...d6.
- [4...d5](/openings/scotch-game-scotch-gambit-goring-gambit-declined), declining, is seen as a strong way for Black to equalise. The critical line runs 5.exd5 Qxd5 6.cxd4 Bg4 7.Be2 Bb4+ 8.Nc3 Bxf3 9.Bxf3 Qc4, often called the Capablanca Variation after Black's play in Marshall–Capablanca, Lake Hopatcong 1926. White must exchange queens into an equal ending or give up castling with the risky 10.Be3, and this line keeps many players away from the gambit.
- 4...Nf6 transposes to a line of the [Ponziani Opening](/openings/ponziani-opening). 4...d3 declines too, but after 5.Bxd3 White has a strong lead in development.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 55.2% of the 2.8 million games and Black 40.7%. Below 2200 White wins 53 to 56%, and from 2200 up the margin shrinks to 48.8% against 44.3%. In 736 master games over the board the two sides are level: White won 31.1%, Black 31.2%, and 37.6% were drawn.
