In the Exchange Variation of the [French Defense](/openings/french-defense) (1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.exd5 exd5) White releases the tension in the centre at once. The pawn structure becomes symmetrical, and Black's light-squared bishop, often the problem piece in the French, is freed.

### Origins

Howard Staunton recommended the Exchange in the 19th century, but it has been in decline ever since. Garry Kasparov briefly experimented with it in the early 1990s before switching to 3.Nc3. Because of the symmetrical structure the line has a reputation for giving Black immediate equality. An extreme example was Capablanca–Maróczy, Lake Hopatcong 1926, agreed drawn after 21 moves.

### The idea

Many 1.e4 players find the closed structures of the French hard to play against, so they choose the Exchange to make the position simpler. White makes no attempt to exploit the first move, and draws are frequent if neither side breaks the symmetry. White cannot force a draw, though: in Tatai–Korchnoi, Beer Sheva 1978, White played for safety and lost in 14 moves, and in Gurevich–Short, Manila 1990, Nigel Short ground down a White player aiming for a draw in 42 moves.

To play for a win White often plays c2–c4 at some point, pressing on d5; Black can then give White an isolated queen's pawn, which frees White's pieces. If White does not play c4, Black may play ...c5 instead. Castling on opposite sides is another way to unbalance the position.

### Main lines

- 4.Nf3, the [most common](/openings/french-defense-exchange-variation-3) fourth move. 4.Nf3 Bd6 5.c4 may transpose to the [Petrov Defense](/openings/petrovs-defense).
- 4.Nc3, the [other main move](/openings/french-defense-exchange-variation-2).
- 4.c4, the [Monte Carlo Variation](/openings/french-defense-exchange-variation-monte-carlo-variation), played by grandmasters Normunds Miezis and Maurice Ashley.
- 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.exd5 exd5 5.Bg5, the [Svenonius Variation](/openings/french-defense-exchange-variation-svenonius-variation), reaches the exchange structure from a different move order.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Avoids the closed, theory-heavy French main lines.
- Simple, symmetrical positions with clear piece placement.

**Weaknesses**

- White gives up the fight for an opening advantage; Black equalises easily.
- Playing only for a draw can backfire, as Tatai–Korchnoi showed.

### Who should play it

The Exchange suits White players who want to avoid French theory and are happy with a quiet, symmetrical game, or who plan an early c4 to unbalance it.

### The numbers

On Lichess the results are nearly even: White 47.1%, Black 46.8%, with 6.0% draws. Under 1400 White leads 49.1% against 46.7% and at 1400–1799 48.6% against 46.6%; at 1800–2199 Black is slightly ahead, 47.1% against 46.6%, and from 2200 up it is 45.7% against 45.3% with 9.0% draws. In about 8,200 master games 55.3% were drawn, and Black won more often than White, 24.8% against 19.9%.
