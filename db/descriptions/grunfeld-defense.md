The Grünfeld Defense (1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6 3.Nc3 d5) is a dynamic, hypermodern answer to 1.d4. Black invites White to build a big pawn centre with cxd5 and e4, and then attacks it with pieces and the ...c5 break, especially with the bishop on g7.

### Origins

The first recorded game with the Grünfeld set-up was played in 1855 by Moheschunder Bannerjee against John Cochrane. The opening is named after the Austrian grandmaster Ernst Grünfeld, who introduced it into international play at the Bad Pistyan tournament in April 1922. The "Game of the Century" between Donald Byrne and 13-year-old Bobby Fischer in 1956 featured it. Garry Kasparov often used it, including in his world championship matches against Anatoly Karpov in 1986, 1987 and 1990. In the 21st century it has been played by elite players such as Peter Svidler and Maxime Vachier-Lagrave.

### The idea

The Grünfeld is a deal: Black gives White the centre and then attacks it. After 4.cxd5 Nxd5 5.e4 Nxc3 6.bxc3, White has a powerful pawn duo on d4 and e4 but a weakened queenside. Black puts pressure on d4 with ...Bg7, ...c5, ...Nc6, ...Qa5 and ...Bg4. If the centre holds, White usually gets a strong attack or a bind. If it cracks, Black's pieces take over, and the queenside endgame often favours Black.

### Main lines

- [Exchange Variation](/openings/grunfeld-defense-exchange-variation) 4.cxd5 Nxd5 5.e4: the critical main line, with the [Modern Exchange](/openings/grunfeld-defense-exchange-variation-modern-exchange-variation) 7.Nf3 and the [Classical](/openings/grunfeld-defense-exchange-variation-classical-variation) 7.Bc4.
- [Russian Variation](/openings/grunfeld-defense-russian-variation) 4.Nf3 Bg7 5.Qb3: White attacks d5 and builds a centre with e4, with Black's queen and queenside counterplay against it.
- [Three Knights Variation](/openings/grunfeld-defense-three-knights-variation) 4.Nf3, with quieter systems like [5.Bf4](/openings/grunfeld-defense-three-knights-variation-hungarian-attack) and [5.e3](/openings/grunfeld-defense-three-knights-variation-burille-variation).
- Anti-Grünfeld tries: the [Brinckmann Attack](/openings/grunfeld-defense-brinckmann-attack) 4.Bf4 and the [Stockholm Variation](/openings/grunfeld-defense-stockholm-variation) 4.Bg5.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Objectively one of the soundest dynamic defences to 1.d4, trusted at world championship level.
- Active piece play and concrete counterplay rather than passive defence.
- Endgames are often good for Black thanks to the queenside majority.

**Weaknesses**

- A lot of forcing, concrete theory, especially in the Exchange Variation.
- If Black misplays the counterattack, White's centre can roll forward and crush Black.
- White can sidestep it with 3.Nf3 and g3, or with 3.f3, so Black needs other lines too.

### Who should play it

The Grünfeld suits well-prepared, concrete players who like dynamic, piece-driven positions and are willing to study theory. It is effective against White players who build big centres by reflex, because the centre becomes a target. It is less comfortable against opponents who choose quiet systems (such as 5.e3) or anti-Grünfeld move orders. Players who want a similar setup but closed centres often prefer the [King's Indian](/openings/kings-indian-defense).

### The numbers

The Grünfeld is one of the few main openings where Black scores better than White on Lichess below 2200: about 48–49% for Black against 46–48% for White. Above 2200, White leads 47% to 44%. In master games over the board, 51% of games are drawn, White wins 29% and Black 20%.
